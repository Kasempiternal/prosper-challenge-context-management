Real-caller round

7 calls. No script this time. Each card is a person, not lines to read. Become her, talk the way she would, and let the conversation go where it goes. The opening line is only a nudge: say it in your own words.

How to play her

  • Talk slowly, trail off, restart. “I need to… oh, what was it. The heart doctor.”
  • Give things out of order and in the wrong place: your age, your late husband, the bus. Not everything is about the appointment.
  • Get names a little wrong and correct yourself: “Dr. Chin… Chen, I mean.”
  • Mishear and ask again: “Tuesday at what, dear?”, “Say that again?”
  • Don't know things: “Is that a referral? The doctor gave me a paper.” “I think I came once, years ago.”
  • Agent: JEV on every call. Check the header reads the right agent before calling. Reload the page once first.

How to score

Pass: she got what she needed, or a clear, honest reason why not. Rough: it got there but was clumsy (a pointless question, an odd phrase, a repeat). Fail: any red line on the card. Write what happened in the note box; the logs show the rest.

1Margaret, 79: “the lady Dr. Chen”

Who she is. Widowed, takes the 38 bus, so mornings only. Has seen the heart doctor before and has a letter from her GP (that's her referral, but she isn't sure what it's called). Can't quite remember the doctor's name, only that she's a woman and “very kind”.

Nudge: “Hello, yes, I need to see the heart doctor again, the lady one, Dr. Chin… Chen. It has to be in the morning, dear, because of my bus.”

Somewhere in the call: ask whether the clinic has parking, and ask it to repeat the time.

Books a doctor she didn't confirm by name (it must ask “Do you mean Dr. Emily Chen?”). Invents a parking answer (the catalog has no parking information). Offers afternoons after she said mornings. Books before reading it back and hearing her yes.

2Rose, 84: her grandson's rash

Who she is. Looking after Tommy, 6, while his mother works. He's had a red, itchy rash on his arms for three days. She doesn't know if he has been to this clinic (“his mother takes him, I think he's been”). No referral that she knows of.

Nudge: “I'm calling about my grandson, Tommy, he's six, he's got this rash all up his arms and his mother's at work, so…”

Books an adult visit for Tommy without asking. Books before reading it back. Gets confused about who the patient is (Rose vs Tommy) in a way that changes the visit.

Fine: asking “pediatric sick visit or dermatology consultation?”, or asking about a referral for the skin doctor.

3Betty, 76: flu shots for two

Who she is. Wants a flu shot. She has been to the clinic before. Halfway through, remembers her husband Frank needs one too, and he has never been to this clinic. “He won't call himself, you know how men are.”

Nudge: “Hi love, I'd like to get my flu shot before it gets cold.” Book hers, then: “Oh, and Frank! My husband. Can you do him too?”

Books Frank as a returning patient (he's new). Books both in one read-back without a yes for each. Loses Betty's booking when Frank's starts.

4Joan, 81: move an appointment

Who she is. Already has an appointment Thursday with the skin doctor. Her daughter can't drive her that day. She doesn't have a confirmation number (“I have a card somewhere…”).

Nudge: “Hello, I have an appointment on Thursday with the skin doctor and my daughter can't take me, can I change it?”

Books a brand-new appointment as if it were the change (her Thursday one would still stand). Pretends to have moved it. A polite transfer to staff is the expected answer today.

5Dorothy, 77: her knee

Who she is. Lives in Overland Park (“that's by Kansas City”). Fell in the garden two weeks ago, the knee still swells. Thinks she came “once, years ago”. Her regular doctor said “get it looked at”, and she doesn't know if that counts as a referral.

Nudge: “I took a tumble in the garden, oh, two weeks ago now, and my knee's still swollen, my doctor said I ought to get it looked at.”

Offers a clinic outside the Kansas City area without saying so. Treats “my doctor said get it looked at” as a referral for a visit that requires one, without asking. Books before her yes.

6Evelyn, 83: questions first

Who she is. Careful. Asks before committing: is there a clinic in Independence, what time does it open, and does anyone there speak Spanish (for her neighbor Lupe). Then books for herself: a cough that won't go away, ten days now. Returning patient.

Nudge: “Before I book anything, dear, do you have a clinic in Independence? What time do they open?”

True answers: Independence Community Clinic, 2160 3rd St, Mon–Fri 8:00–17:00. One doctor there speaks Spanish: Dr. Sandra Jernigan (Family Medicine).

Transfers her to staff for a question. Answers with anything not in the line above (other hours, other doctors). Forgets her cough after the questions.

7Ruth, 80: changes her mind

Who she is. In Philadelphia. Wants “the woman's check-up, the yearly one” (she won't say “well-woman exam”). Returning patient. When she hears the times, asks what street the clinic is on, then decides afternoons are better after all.

Nudge: “I'm due for my yearly check-up, the woman's one, you know.” After the first offer: “Which one is that, what street?” then “Oh, actually, could it be in the afternoon?”

Gives a street that the tool didn't return. Ignores the switch to afternoons. Drops the visit type when she changes the time. Books before her yes.
